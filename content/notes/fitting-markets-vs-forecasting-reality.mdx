A calibrated options model and a weather model look similar from a
distance: equations, parameters, data, fit. But they are doing opposite
jobs, and much confusion in quantitative finance comes from grading one
by the other's rubric.

## The interpolation job

When a desk calibrates SVI to today's option quotes, the model is a
_consistency device_. Its purpose is to price the instrument you don't
observe from the ones you do — coherently, without internal arbitrage.
Nobody believes the parameters describe how the asset actually moves.
The model is judged on fit, smoothness, and no-arbitrage, and it is
re-fit tomorrow without embarrassment when the parameters jump.

This is closer to cartography than physics. A map of today's prices does
not claim the terrain will hold still.

## The forecasting job

A forecast makes a claim about the world outside the quote screen: this
is how much the asset will move, this is where the pollutant plume goes,
this is the stress the material will bear. Now the parameters _are_
supposed to mean something, stability across refits is evidence rather
than aesthetics, and out-of-sample error — not fit — is the score.

<MarginNote index={1}>
  The same equation can do either job. Black–Scholes as interpolator is a quoting
  convention; Black–Scholes as forecast is a (bad) claim about return distributions.
</MarginNote>

## Where the confusion bites

- **Backtesting a calibration.** A surface model that reprices today's
  quotes to a tenth of a vol point has demonstrated nothing about
  tomorrow. Fit quality is not evidence of predictive power.
- **Forecasting with the market's numbers.** Implied volatility is a
  price, and prices embed premia. Using it raw as a forecast
  systematically over-predicts realized movement in most regimes.
- **Parameter storytelling.** Narrating the daily wiggles of calibrated
  parameters as if they were state variables of reality — when they are
  partly just the model absorbing what it cannot express.

## The discipline

Before building anything, I try to write one sentence: _is this model
supposed to agree with prices, or with the future?_ The answer changes
the loss function, the validation design, and what "wrong" even means.
The models I trust least are the ones whose builders never chose.
