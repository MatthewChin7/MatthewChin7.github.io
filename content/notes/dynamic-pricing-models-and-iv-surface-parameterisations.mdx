Black–Scholes, Heston, jump diffusion, Dupire, SVI/SSVI, and heck, the Bates
model. When I first learned about different pricing models and
volatility-surface interpolation techniques, I came across far too many
trivialisations that I found confusing. Worse, I became convinced that these
were simply different ways to interpolate a volatility surface. I wrote this
short article as the resource I wish I had before constructing my first
volatility surface.

## Mistake 1. Market option prices form a complete, smooth surface

Retrospectively, I am not sure how I came to make this assumption, and I might
be unique in this blunder. Either way, the mistake is trivially explainable.
Not all option markets are replete with quotes for _every_ strike and maturity;
you would be hard-pressed to find a large market for extremely out-of-the-money
options. Moreover, observed implied volatilities are not necessarily
distributed smoothly across strikes and maturities. Even for a single $(K,T)$,
there may be different quotes because of bid–ask spreads, trading venues, or
data sources. Effective smoothing and interpolation methods are therefore
required to construct a coherent surface.

## Mistake 2. Interpolation across strikes and tenors is a single endeavour

Again, perhaps a unique shortcoming, but interpolating across strikes is
different from interpolating across tenors. Techniques such as SVI allow us to
interpolate across log-forward moneyness _per tenor_, whereas SSVI provides a
parameterisation across both moneyness _and_ tenor. Technically, fitting an
independent SVI slice with five parameters to each of $n$ maturities gives $5n$
parameters before any cross-tenor constraints are imposed.

## Mistake 3. All the different volatility models are merely different interpolation methods

Unfortunately, no. The objects discussed above broadly fall into two
categories: dynamic pricing models and surface parameterisations.

Dynamic pricing models include Black–Scholes, Heston, jump diffusion, Dupire,
and, for the expert modeller, the Bates model. I found it especially tempting
to use Bates when I first started volatility modelling; I was left with an
overfitted pie in the face. These models are used to price options and carry
different assumptions about the dynamics of the underlying and its volatility.

_Constant volatility (Black–Scholes):_ volatility for a given underlying is
fixed.

_Local volatility (Dupire):_ volatility varies _deterministically_ with the
underlying spot $S_t$ and time $t$.

_Stochastic volatility (Heston):_ volatility is itself a random process driven
by a separate Brownian shock. In Heston, variance follows a square-root,
mean-reverting process of the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross form.

_Jump diffusion:_ the underlying price experiences random jumps, typically
governed by a Poisson process. In the standard Merton jump-diffusion model, the
diffusion volatility may remain constant; it is the underlying price, rather
than volatility itself, that jumps.

_Stochastic volatility with jumps (Bates):_ Bates combines Heston-style
stochastic volatility with jumps in the underlying price.

Surface parameterisations, by contrast, allow us to represent a smooth
implied-volatility surface in terms of a finite collection of parameters. SVI
parameterises an individual strike slice, while SSVI links the smile across
both strike and maturity. SABR occupies a slightly different position: it is a
stochastic-volatility model whose approximate implied-volatility formula is
also commonly used to parameterise individual smile slices. Independent strike
slices must be combined with an effective tenor interpolation or
parameterisation and appropriate no-arbitrage constraints.

## Mistake 3.5. Implied volatility is simply the risk-neutral expectation of all possible local-volatility paths

Formally, the standard Black–Scholes implied volatility
$\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K,T)$ is the single constant volatility that makes the
Black–Scholes price equal to the observed market price of an option with strike
$K$ and maturity $T$. In general,

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(K,T)
\neq
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}
\left[
\frac{1}{T}
\int_0^T \sigma_\text{loc}^2(S_t,t)\,dt
\right].
$$

The expression on the right is the expected average realised variance under
$\mathbb{Q}$ and, under the usual continuous-diffusion assumptions, corresponds
to the fair variance-swap rate. Unlike implied volatility, it is not
strike-dependent.

Implied volatility is instead an option-specific, constant-volatility summary
of how the entire risk-neutral distribution of the future underlying price
affects that option's value. If the local-volatility model has been calibrated
to the observed surface, then

$$
C_{\mathrm{BS}}
\left(S_0,K,T,\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K,T)\right)
=
\exp(-rT)
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\mathbb{Q}}
\left[(S_T-K)^+\right],
$$

where the local-volatility dynamics are

$$
dS_t
=
(r-q)S_t\,dt
+
\sigma_\text{loc}(S_t,t)S_t\,dW_t^\mathbb{Q}.
$$

By comparison, under Black–Scholes,

$$
S_T
=
S_0
\exp
\left[
\left(r-q-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2\right)T
+
\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}\sqrt{T}Z
\right],
\qquad
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
$$

Thus, $\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K,T)$ is chosen so that this lognormal model
reproduces the option price generated by the relevant risk-neutral
distribution; it is not generally an average of the local-volatility paths.

## Mistake 4. Risk-neutral and physical measures describe the same expectation

The physical measure $\mathbb{P}$ describes the distribution of outcomes we
expect to observe in the real world. Thus, it is relevant for forecasting
realised returns and volatility. The risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, by
contrast, is the pricing measure under which discounted tradable asset prices
are martingales. It is inferred from market prices and incorporates the risk
premia investors require to bear uncertainty.

Consequently, option-implied volatility should not be interpreted as a pure
forecast of future realised volatility. Schematically,

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[RV_{0,T}]
=
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[RV_{0,T}]
+
\text{variance risk premium},
$$

where the sign of the final term follows the convention used here. Implied
quantities combine expectations about future outcomes with the market price of
the risks associated with those outcomes.
