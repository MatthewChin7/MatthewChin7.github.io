## A brief explanation of local volatility

Local volatility gives the instantaneous underlying volatility
$\sigma_\text{loc}(S_t,t)$, where $S_t$ is the underlying spot at time $t$.
Unlike Black–Scholes implied volatility $\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K,T)$, local
volatility does not depend on the particular option used to _express_ the
underlying; every option is priced using the same instantaneous underlying
local-volatility function. Hence, local volatility provides a state-dependent
description of the underlying's instantaneous volatility, which should not
itself depend on the option being priced.

Implied volatility is the constant Black–Scholes volatility that reproduces an
option's market price, whereas local volatility describes the _instantaneous_
volatility implied by the entire option surface. Schematically, the reader may
find the following heuristic helpful:

$$
\text{local variance}
\xrightarrow[\text{aggregate across paths and time}]{\mathbb{Q}}
\text{option-implied variance}.
$$

For a variance-swap-style measure of implied variance, this relationship can be
written more precisely as

$$
\text{implied variance}
\approx
\mathbb{E}_0^\mathbb{Q}[RV_{0,T}]
=
\mathbb{E}_0^\mathbb{P}[RV_{0,T}]
+
\text{variance risk premium},
$$

where $\mathbb{E}_0^\mathbb{P}[RV_{0,T}]$ is the physical expected realised
variance from now ($t=0$) to maturity $T$. The final equality uses the
convention that the variance risk premium is the risk-neutral expectation minus
the physical expectation.

## The Dupire Equation

The Dupire Equation is the standard way to infer local volatility from a
complete European option surface. It is the volatility that satisfies the
diffusion

$$
dS_t
=
(r-q)S_t\,dt
+
\sigma_\text{loc}(S_t,t)S_t\,dW_t^\mathbb{Q},
$$

where $r$ is the constant risk-free rate, $q$ is the dividend yield, and
$dW_t^\mathbb{Q}$ is the Brownian increment under risk-neutral dynamics.
Solving the corresponding forward equation for a European call price $C$,
strike $K$, and maturity $T$ gives

$$
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)
=
\frac{
\partial_T C+(r-q)K\partial_K C+qC
}{
\frac{1}{2}K^2\partial_{KK}C
}.
$$

[^coords]

[^coords]:
    The keen-eyed reader may notice that local volatility is given here as a
    function of $K$ and $T$, unlike its normal expression as a function of $S_t$
    and $t$. We must _calibrate_ local volatility as $\sigma_\text{loc}(K,T)$
    because we are using a market-implied option surface indexed by strike and
    maturity. However, _after_ calibration, we reinterpret the coordinates
    $(K,T)$ as the future state and time $(S_t,t)$. Hence,
    $\sigma_\text{loc}(S_t,t)$ is the same numerical surface evaluated with
    differently named coordinates.

For zero rates and dividends, Dupire simplifies to its more recognised form:

$$
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)
=
\frac{2\partial_T C}{K^2\partial_{KK}C}.
$$

Clearly, ensuring a _smooth_ and _complete_ option-price surface in tenor and
strike is necessary to calculate $\partial_T C$ and $\partial_{KK}C$. For
sparse and noisy market option prices, we would first need to fit a smooth,
arbitrage-free Black–Scholes implied volatility surface, such as SVI or SSVI,
to market quotes (which may themselves be quoted in tenor and delta) before
applying Dupire. This fitting procedure itself introduces further modelling
assumptions. I find the following rubric helpful:

$$
\text{observed option prices}
\longrightarrow
\text{BS implied vol}
\longrightarrow
\text{SVI/SSVI surface}
\longrightarrow
\text{Dupire local vol}.
$$

Like Black–Scholes, Heston, and jump-diffusion models, the local-volatility
model is a dynamic pricing model. However, unlike Heston and jump-diffusion
models, its volatility is a _deterministic_ function of the underlying's spot
and time; unlike Black–Scholes volatility, it is _state-dependent_.

## Derivation

I find the following derivation to be the most intuitive and simple, although
there are other equally rigorous and valid proofs. Feel free to comment with
any better formulations.

The general idea is to use the forward Kolmogorov equation to connect the
risk-neutral probability density to the option-price surface, and then express
this density in terms of derivatives of the option price to obtain local
volatility:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{local-volatility SDE}
\longrightarrow
\text{forward Kolmogorov equation}
\longrightarrow
\text{risk-neutral density}\\
\longrightarrow
\text{call-price derivatives}
\longrightarrow
\text{Dupire equation}
\end{gathered}
}
$$

First, the risk-neutral local-volatility diffusion is given by

$$
dS_t
=
(r-q)S_t\,dt
+
\sigma_\text{loc}(S_t,t)S_t\,dW_t^\mathbb{Q}.
$$

Let

$$
f(s,t)
$$

be the risk-neutral PDF of $S_t=s$. We know that, for a general diffusion

$$
dX_t
=
\mu(X_t,t)\,dt
+
a(X_t,t)\,dW_t,
$$

the forward Kolmogorov equation[^kolmogorov] is

[^kolmogorov]:
    For a full treatment, see
    [this derivation by Oliver Bühler](https://cims.nyu.edu/~obuhler/Oliver_Buhler/Teaching_12_Spring_files/PDEfinance12notes1.pdf).

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
=
-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\mu f)
+
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}(a^2f).
$$

Applying the forward Kolmogorov equation, where

$$
\mu=(r-q)s,
\qquad
a=\sigma_\text{loc}(s,T)s,
$$

yields

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}
=
-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}
\left[(r-q)sf(s,T)\right]
+
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial s^2}
\left[
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(s,T)s^2f(s,T)
\right].
$$

Next, for a European call with strike $K$ and maturity $T$, its price is given
by the present value of its risk-neutral expected payoff:

$$
C(K,T)
=
\exp(-rT)
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(S_T-K)^+\right],
$$

which we can rewrite in terms of the PDF $f$ as

$$
C(K,T)
=
\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty (s-K)f(s,T)\,ds.
$$

Slightly cheating, we know that the Dupire Equation involves $\partial_T C$ and
$\partial_{KK}C$, so naturally, it makes sense to differentiate the above
expression with respect to $T$ and twice with respect to $K$.

### 1. Differentiate w.r.t. $K$

$$
\partial_K C
=
\exp(-rT)
\frac{\partial}{\partial K}
\int_K^\infty (s-K)f(s,T)\,ds.
$$

By Leibniz's integral rule (otherwise known as DUThIS, ifykyk),

$$
\partial_K C
=
-\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty f(s,T)\,ds.
$$

The integral is the risk-neutral survival probability evaluated at $K$, so

$$
\partial_K C
=
-\exp(-rT)
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Q}}(S_T>K).
$$

Differentiating again yields the extremely useful and famous
Breeden–Litzenberger relation:

$$
\partial_{KK}C
=
\exp(-rT)f(K,T).
$$

### 2. Differentiate w.r.t. $T$

This is slightly more difficult because of the dependence on $T$ in $f(s,T)$.
As before,

$$
\partial_T C
=
\frac{\partial}{\partial T}
\left[
\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty (s-K)f(s,T)\,ds
\right],
$$

which we can evaluate with the product rule:

$$
\partial_T C
=
-rC
+
\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty
(s-K)\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}\,ds.
$$

Substituting the forward Kolmogorov expression for $\partial_T f$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_T C
={}&-rC\\
&\underbrace{
-(r-q)\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty
(s-K)
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}
\left[sf(s,T)\right]\,ds
}_{\text{drift term}}\\
&+
\underbrace{
\frac{1}{2}\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty
(s-K)
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial s^2}
\left[
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(s,T)s^2f(s,T)
\right]\,ds
}_{\text{diffusion term}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 2.1. Drift integral

Integrating by parts once, and assuming the relevant boundary terms vanish as
$s\to\infty$, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-\int_K^\infty
(s-K)
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}
\left[sf(s,T)\right]\,ds\\
&\quad=
-\left[
(s-K)sf(s,T)
\right]_K^\infty
+
\int_K^\infty sf(s,T)\,ds\\
&\quad=
\int_K^\infty sf(s,T)\,ds.
\end{aligned}
$$

We can split $s=(s-K)+K$:

$$
\int_K^\infty sf(s,T)\,ds
=
\int_K^\infty (s-K)f(s,T)\,ds
+
K\int_K^\infty f(s,T)\,ds.
$$

Using the expressions for $C$ and $\partial_K C$,

$$
\exp(-rT)
\int_K^\infty sf(s,T)\,ds
=
C-K\partial_K C.
$$

Therefore, the drift term is

$$
(r-q)\left(C-K\partial_K C\right).
$$

#### 2.2. Diffusion integral

Define

$$
g(s,T)
=
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(s,T)s^2f(s,T).
$$

Integrating by parts twice, again assuming the relevant boundary terms vanish,
gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_K^\infty
(s-K)\frac{\partial^2g}{\partial s^2}\,ds
&=
\left[
(s-K)\frac{\partial g}{\partial s}
\right]_K^\infty
-
\int_K^\infty
\frac{\partial g}{\partial s}\,ds\\
&=
-\left[g(s,T)\right]_K^\infty\\
&=
g(K,T).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the diffusion term is

$$
\frac{1}{2}\exp(-rT)
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)K^2f(K,T).
$$

By the Breeden–Litzenberger relation,

$$
\exp(-rT)f(K,T)
=
\partial_{KK}C,
$$

so the diffusion term becomes

$$
\frac{1}{2}
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)K^2\partial_{KK}C.
$$

Combining the drift and diffusion terms, we obtain

$$
\partial_T C
=
-rC
+
(r-q)\left(C-K\partial_K C\right)
+
\frac{1}{2}
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)K^2\partial_{KK}C.
$$

Since

$$
-rC+(r-q)C=-qC,
$$

this becomes

$$
\partial_T C
=
-qC
-
(r-q)K\partial_K C
+
\frac{1}{2}
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)K^2\partial_{KK}C.
$$

Rearranging finally gives the Dupire Equation:

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_\text{loc}^2(K,T)
=
\frac{
\partial_T C+(r-q)K\partial_K C+qC
}{
\frac{1}{2}K^2\partial_{KK}C
}
}.
$$

Finally, after calibrating local volatility on the $(K,T)$ grid, we reinterpret
strike as the possible future spot state and maturity as future time:

$$
\sigma_\text{loc}(s,t)
=
\left.
\sigma_\text{loc}(K,T)
\right|_{K=s,\,T=t}.
$$

This gives the instantaneous volatility used when the underlying reaches state
$S_t=s$ at time $t$.

## Use

My trading desk did not seem especially keen on local volatility, and indeed,
this article is much more an academic exercise than a practical guide.

Fundamentally, local volatility allows us to express instantaneous volatility
as a function of the underlying spot $S_t$ and time $t$. This gives us the
flexibility to price path-dependent and exotic options within a model that
remains consistent with the observed European vanilla surface. Black–Scholes
can also be used to price some exotic options, but its assumption of constant
volatility prevents it from reproducing the observed volatility smile.

Unlike realised volatility, which is calculated from an observed path under the
physical measure $\mathbb{P}$, local volatility is inferred from option prices
under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$. It therefore gives us an
instantaneous volatility function which, under the local-volatility diffusion,
reproduces the entire observed (or SVI-interpolated) European option surface.

It is important to note that we use the European option surface only to
_calibrate_ Dupire local volatility to observed vanilla prices. This _does not_
mean that an option subsequently priced under the model must itself be
European. Once the local-volatility function has been calibrated, we can
simulate the resulting risk-neutral dynamics and use them to price other,
potentially path-dependent, payoffs. Hence, we can think of Dupire as a
convenient mathematical tool for tuning a particular model of the underlying's
risk-neutral dynamics to observed European option prices, which can then be
applied to other options.

## Limitations

The principal limitation of local volatility is that fitting today's European
option surface does not ensure that the model correctly describes the
underlying's future dynamics. Dupire guarantees that, for every calibrated
maturity $T$, the model reproduces the market-implied marginal risk-neutral
distribution of $S_T$. In other words, if the input surface is complete and
arbitrage-free, the model does _not_ predict the wrong terminal PDF at a
calibrated maturity. However, vanilla prices determine only these marginal
distributions; they do not uniquely determine the joint distribution of the
underlying across time, its transition probabilities, or the paths taken
between today and maturity. Local volatility imposes one particular set of path
dynamics among many models that could fit exactly the same vanilla surface.

This distinction is relatively unimportant for European options, whose payoffs
depend only on $S_T$, but is crucial for exotic options whose payoffs depend on
the path of the underlying. Such options may be mispriced even when the
local-volatility model fits every observed vanilla option perfectly. Hence,
while local volatility "one-ups" Black–Scholes by reproducing the vanilla smile
within a model that can price exotics, its path and smile dynamics may be less
realistic than those of stochastic-volatility models such as Heston.

A related problem is that local volatility generally produces unrealistic smile
dynamics. Because volatility is a deterministic function of spot and time,

$$
\sigma_t=\sigma_\text{loc}(S_t,t),
$$

the model contains no source of volatility randomness independent of the
underlying. Conditional on time, volatility changes are mechanically linked to
changes in spot, so the model cannot represent independent volatility shocks or
stochastic changes in the shape of the smile. In practice, the future
implied-volatility surface may therefore evolve very differently from the
dynamics implied by today's local-volatility surface.

Local volatility is also numerically unstable because it requires
$\partial_T C$, $\partial_K C$, and especially $\partial_{KK}C$, all of which
amplify noise. Hence, small errors in observed prices or in the fitted SVI/SSVI
surface can create large changes in the inferred local volatility. A smooth and
arbitrage-free input surface is therefore essential for obtaining stable
results.

Furthermore, an exact fit to current vanilla prices does not imply that the
model will remain calibrated. As spot, time, and market expectations change,
the observed implied-volatility surface also changes. A local-volatility
surface calibrated today is deterministic and does not automatically adapt to
these new market conditions; in practice, it must be recalibrated frequently.
Consequently, local volatility may hedge vanilla options reasonably well
against small spot movements while producing unreliable vega, volga, and
forward-smile exposures for more complex products.

Finally, the standard Dupire model assumes continuous diffusion dynamics,
deterministic rates and dividends, and no jumps. These assumptions may be
inadequate for assets subject to earnings announcements, defaults, policy
decisions, or other discontinuous moves. Extensions can incorporate these
factors, although these would be different, and more complicated, local
volatility models.
