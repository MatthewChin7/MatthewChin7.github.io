Traders quote both in the same units — annualized percent — which invites
treating them as two estimates of one quantity. They are not. Realized
volatility is a backward-looking sample statistic; implied volatility is a
forward-looking price. The gap between them is not an error to be
arbitraged away by better measurement; it is itself a traded risk premium.

## Two different objects

Realized volatility over a window of $n$ daily returns is a statistic:

$$
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{RV}} = \sqrt{\frac{252}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i^2}
$$

It answers: _how much did this asset actually move?_ Its only randomness
is sampling error, and you can shrink that with higher-frequency data
(up to the point where microstructure noise bites back).

ATM implied volatility is the number you feed into Black–Scholes to
recover an option's market price. It answers a different question
entirely: _what constant volatility makes the model agree with what
people are paying?_ It is a price restated in volatility units — and
prices embed risk preferences, hedging demand, and inventory, not just
forecasts.

<MarginNote index={1}>
  This is the P-versus-Q distinction: realized vol lives under the physical measure,
  implied vol under the risk-neutral one. Conflating them is a category error before it is
  an empirical one.
</MarginNote>

## Why they disagree structurally

If option sellers were risk-neutral statisticians, implied variance would
equal expected realized variance. Empirically, index implied volatility
sits above subsequent realized volatility most of the time. Sellers of
volatility are short convexity in exactly the states where losses hurt
most, and they charge for it. The difference — the variance risk premium
— is compensation, not mispricing.

Three further wedges keep the two numbers apart even in expectation:

- **Horizon.** Implied vol is an expectation over the option's remaining
  life; realized vol is measured over some past window. In a regime
  change, they _should_ disagree.
- **The smile.** "ATM implied vol" is one point on a curve. The rest of
  the smile encodes skewness and kurtosis the single point discards; a
  realized measure has no strike dimension at all.
- **Jumps and gaps.** Close-to-close realized measures under-sample gap
  risk; option prices cannot afford to.

## The practical upshot

Comparing this month's realized vol against today's implied vol tells you
about the _current price of volatility risk_, not about which number is
"right." A model that fits the implied surface can be flawless as a
description of prices and useless as a forecast of movement — and vice
versa. That distinction has its own note: fitting the market is not
forecasting reality.
