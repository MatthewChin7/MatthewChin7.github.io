## Abstract

Built for a live trading competition: a Python system that quoted
two-sided markets in several instruments, priced derivatives off its own
mid estimates, and enforced position and loss limits mechanically. The
interesting engineering was not the pricing formulas but the plumbing —
staying responsive while the exchange feed, the pricing loop, and the
risk checks all wanted the same milliseconds.

## Architecture

- **Feed handler** — normalizes exchange messages into an internal book.
- **Fair-value engine** — mid estimates blended from book imbalance and
  recent trades; derivatives priced off those mids rather than last trades.
- **Quoter** — spreads set from inventory and recent volatility; quotes
  widen mechanically as position accumulates (a standard
  inventory-control policy in the spirit of Avellaneda–Stoikov).
- **Risk layer** — hard position caps and a kill switch that pulls all
  quotes; the one component allowed to interrupt every other.

<Callout kind="idea">
  A market maker's edge is mostly the discipline of its exits: the system design question
  is "what turns quoting off?", not "what turns it on?"
</Callout>

## What failed or changed

Early versions re-priced on every book tick and fell behind during bursts;
batching repricing onto a fixed cadence made behavior predictable under
load. Adverse selection was the persistent tax: naive symmetric quotes
around a stale mid get hit precisely when the mid is most wrong.

## What I would do next

Replace the hand-tuned spread policy with one calibrated from the
competition's own fill data, and measure the adverse-selection cost per
fill directly.
