## Abstract

Hong Kong's pollution field is shaped by wind funneled through steep
terrain, dense street canyons, and marine boundary effects. This project
builds an hourly spatial model of pollutant concentration on a grid of
roughly 500-meter resolution, driven by an advection–diffusion equation
and constrained by monitoring-station measurements, meteorological data,
and GIS layers.

## The physical model

The concentration field $c(x, y, t)$ evolves under

$$
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}
  + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c
  = \nabla\cdot\left(K\,\nabla c\right) + S - \lambda c
$$

where $\mathbf{u}$ is the horizontal wind field, $K$ an eddy-diffusivity
tensor, $S$ emission sources, and $\lambda$ a first-order removal
term.<MarginNote index="1">The interesting modeling decisions are all in
what this equation hides: vertical mixing collapsed into $K$, chemistry
collapsed into $\lambda$.</MarginNote>

## Data

- Hourly measurements from Hong Kong's air-quality monitoring network.
- Meteorological reanalysis and station wind fields, interpolated to the grid.
- GIS layers — terrain elevation, land use, road density — informing the
  source term and local diffusivity.

## Method

Finite-volume discretization on the 500 m grid with upwinding for the
advective term; source and diffusivity parameters fit against held-out
stations rather than assumed. The honest difficulty is identifiability:
with tens of stations and thousands of cells, the data cannot pin down a
full parameter field, so the parameterization must stay low-dimensional.

## What I would do next

Compare the physical model against a purely statistical interpolator
(kriging with meteorological covariates) on identical held-out stations —
the cleanest test of whether the physics earns its complexity.
